\section{Finding 3: The Template Attack}
\label{sec:f3}

\subsection{Existence and attribution failure}
Texts assembled from repeated template sentences are a real document class:
form letters, mail merges, generated reviews, compliance checklists. We
construct them by repeating a four-sentence Chinese template with
zero-cost-dictionary saturation ($6$--$10$ copies; 100 texts) and measure
both failure modes of a verifier with a 50-user registry: existence FPR and
\emph{false attribution} (a specific wrong user returned with confidence).

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig_eb6_template_attribution.pdf}
\caption{E-B6: template texts vs.\ registry size (1--200 users). Existence
FPR is high throughout (73--79\%); attribution FPR is substantial
(1--46\%) but not monotone, while the mechanism quantities---candidate
score ratio and band-alignment rate---rise monotonically with registry
size.}
\label{fig:eb6}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:eb6} (existence/attribution) shows the surface phenomenon;
the lower panel quantities reveal the mechanism. The winning candidate's
score ratio (best soft-match score divided by the theoretical maximum
$\sum|z|$) rises monotonically with registry size
($-0.02 \to 0.30 \to 0.53 \to 0.64$ for 1/10/50/200 users), as does the
fraction of bands whose sign agrees with the winner's bit pattern
($0.50 \to 0.64 \to 0.76 \to 0.81$). The template's genuine statistical
structure---a few dominant bands with consistent colors---\emph{really
aligns} with some registered user's bit pattern as the registry grows.
This is why abstain-on-low-margin protocols fail here: the margin is not
low. The protocol's truth-absence guard (winner score must reach a fraction
of the theoretical maximum) is calibrated for random noise and is bypassed
by real alignment.

Attribution FPR itself is not monotone in registry size (14\%, 46\%, 13\%,
1\% at 1/10/50/200 users across two independent 100-text samples, 13--28\%):
it additionally depends on the masked $k$-bit collision structure of the
specific registry. The mechanism quantities, not the headline rate, are the
predictable signature.

\subsection{Natural templates and mitigation ranking}
Pure sentence copying is the extreme of a spectrum. We therefore test
\emph{slot-filled} templates (names/topics drawn from pools; sentence
uniqueness 0.47--0.76; 100 texts). False attribution disappears entirely
(0\% in all modes)---extreme repetition is a necessary condition for
misattribution---but existence FPR remains 30\% uncalibrated, dropping to
2\% under in-domain calibration. A business-layer prefilter (reject texts
with unique-sentence ratio below 0.5) rejects 12\% of natural templates
(only the extreme bucket) and leaves 4.6\% FPR in the passed set.

Table~\ref{tab:eb7} ranks the mitigations. For $z$-test schemes, the
template attack is a \emph{structural boundary}: the same
vocabulary-dense, low-entropy construction that makes templates ubiquitous
in business workflows makes them statistically adversarial, and no protocol
tweak repairs this---only calibration on the target domain or routing
highly repetitive texts away from watermark verdicts contain the damage.

\subsection{Statistic dependence, again}
Running the same construction through PostMark's official detector yields
presence scores of exactly 0.0 on all 200 template texts: the
embedding-derived word lists for a repetitive text consist of
semantically-related words that do not occur in the text, and literal
presence fails. PostMark is structurally immune to this template class---an
instance of the general pattern that bounded, per-text-adaptive statistics
trade away the UID-decoding capacity that $z$-test schemes use, and with it
F1/F3 exposure (\S\ref{sec:recs}).
